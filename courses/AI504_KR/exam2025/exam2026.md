## Open Question 1
> Consider the following list of five sentences.
>
> (a) Sentences (e) and (b) disagree.
> (b) Sentences (a) and (c) disagree.
> (c) Sentences (b) and (d) disagree.
> (d) Sentences (c) and (e) disagree.
> (e) Sentences (d) and (a) disagree.
>
> A *solution* is an assignment of a boolean value to each of the five sentences such that conditions (a)-(e) are satisfied. That is to say, if the boolean value assigned to a particular sentence is *true*, the two adjacent sentences should *disagree*, i.e., have different truth values. If the boolean value assigned to a particular sentences is *false*, then the two adjacent sentences should *agree*, i.e., have the same truth value.

How many solutions are there, and what are they?

(*Hint.* This of course can be rephrased a question asking which models satisfy a particular sentence of propositional logic. Formalizing it as such may—or may not—be useful.)

### Answer
There are five sentence letters here p1, . . . , p5. Sentence (i) says $p_i \leftrightarrow \neg(p_{i-1} \leftrightarrow p_{i+1})$ for

   $1 \leq i \leq 5$, and the question asks us to enumerate the models satisfying the conjunction of these 5

   sentences. There are 32 models, which is probably small enough to brute-force it.

   However there is a more elegant solution. The trick is to express $p_{i+1}$ as a function of $p_{i-1}$ and

   $p_i$, instead of $p_i$ as a function of $p_{i+1}$ and $p_{i-1}$. Now if $p_i$ is false, it says that $p_{i+1}$ is the same as

   $p_{i-1}$, and if $p_i$ is true, it says that $p_{i+1}$ is different from $p_{i-1}$, which gives us the following table:

   $p_{i-1}$ $p_i$ $p_{i+1}$

   $F$ $F$ $F$

   $F$ $T$ $T$

   $T$ $F$ $T$

   $T$ $T$ $F$

   The advantage of this is that we can $p_1$ and $p_2$ and then go around the circle applying this rule to the

   previous two values to get the next one. If we end back where we started, we got a satisfying model.

   So if $p_1, p_2$ is $F,F$, then we get the pattern $F,F,F$. . . and one satisfying model. If $p_1, p_2$ is any of the other

   initial values, we get a pattern repeating two T’s and one F in a cycle of three, e.g, $F,T,T,F,T,T,F,T,T,. . .$ —

   the only difference is where in the cycle we start. But this will not end up in a satisfying model unless the number of sentence letters is a multiple of three. So in the end, we get one satisfying model—all false.

> Work in the logic $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{RC})$ and over the signature with two nouns, c and d, and one verb r.
>
> Let $\mathcal{M}$ be any model in this signature satisfying the following two properties:
> - The denotation of any term that ends in d is nonempty, i.e., $[\![\text{d}]\!]$, $[\![\text{r all d}]\!]$, $[\![\text{r all (r all d)}]\!]$, ... are all nonempty subsets of $M$.
> - $[\![\text{r}]\!]$ is a transitive relation, i.e., for any $x, y, z \in M$, if $(x, y) \in [\![\text{r}]\!]$ and $(y, z) \in [\![\text{r}]\!]$ then $(x, z) \in [\![\text{r}]\!]$.

(a) Suppose that $\mathcal{M} \models$ All c (r all (r all d)). Prove that $\mathcal{M} \models$ All c (r all d).

*Hint.* $\mathcal{M} \models$ All c (r all d) means $(\forall x \in [\![\text{c}]\!])(\forall y \in [\![\text{d}]\!])(x, y) \in [\![\text{r}]\!]$. So fix $x \in [\![\text{c}]\!]$ and $y \in [\![\text{d}]\!]$; now your goal is to prove $(x, y) \in [\![\text{r}]\!]$.

(b) Construct a model $\mathcal{N}$ in the same signature that does *not* satisfy these properties, such that $\mathcal{N} \models$ All c (r all (r all d)) but $\mathcal{N} \not\models$ All c (r all d).

(*Hint.* There are tiny models which work. List the elements of the domain and the denotations of c, d, r. From these derive the denotations of r all d and r all (r all d).)

### Answer
For part (a), fix $x \in [[c]]$ and $y \in [[d]]$ as indicated. By the first bullet point, there is $z \in [[r all d]]$.

   And since $M |=$ All c (r all (r all d)), $x \in [[r all (r all d)]]$. In particular this means that $(x, z) \in [[r]]$.

   Since $z \in [[r all d]]$ and $y \in [[d]]$, $(z, y) \in [[r]]$. Since $(x, z)$ and $(z, y) \in [[r]]$, $(x, y) \in [[r]]$, as the relation is

   transitive. But this is what we wanted to prove.

   For part (b) simply let $N$ be any model in which $[[c]], [[d]]$ are nonempty subsets of the domain and $[[r]]$

   is the empty relation. Then $[[r all d]]$ is empty whereas $[[r all (r all d)]]$ is the entire domain. This means

   that $N |=$ All c (r all (r all d)) but $N̸ |=$ All c (r all d).

## Open Question 3
> Work in the logic $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{RC})$ and over the signature with two nouns, c and d, and one verb r.
>
> It is *not* true that $\{\text{All c (r all d)}\} \vdash$ All (r all c) d. For example, we could observe that $\{\text{All c (r all d)}\} \not\models$ All (r all c) d (there are very simple counter-models), then use soundness.
>
> The point of this exercise is to establish $\{\text{All c (r all d)}\} \not\vdash$ All (r all c) d using *entirely proof-theoretic methods*, and avoid discussion of models entirely.

Your task is therefore to define a property of formulas such that:
- All (r all c) d *has* this property,
- All c (r all d) does *not have* this property,
- *no* sentence of the form "All $x$ $x$" has this property, and
- if $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ and $\varphi$ has this property, then at least one sentence in $\Gamma$ has this property. (This will use induction on proof trees in $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{RC})$, it is your choice whether to work with the rule DOWN or ANTI.)

Define such a property of formulas and prove each of these four bullet points. There are *really simple* properties that will work!

### Answer
For example, we could define a property of All s t like “r occurs in s but not t.” This clearly

   satisfies the first three bullet points. For the last, we need to show that if a sentence with this property
   is the conclusion of one of the proof rules, then at least one of the premises must have this property
   too. I’m going just write the crucial observation here.
Here’s the neat thing: a sentence with this property cannot follow from either ANTI or DOWN, because
any conclusion of those rules must have a verb in the second term! So the only possible rule such a sentence
can follow from is BARBARA!
OK, so suppose All s t is the conclusion of a BARBARA rule and has the property that r occurs in s
but not t. Then there is a term x such that the hypotheses are All s x and All x t. Break up into cases
depending on whether x contains an occurrence of r or not. If so, then All x t satisfies this property
(an occurrence of r in the first term but not the second). If not, then All s x satsifies this property. In
either case one of the premises satisfies this property, so we’re done.

## Open Question 4
> Work in the logic $\mathcal{A}$ and the signature 1,2,3. Suppose $\varphi$ is a formula such that
> - $\{\text{All } 1\, 2, \text{All } 2\, 3\} \models \varphi$ and
> - $\{\text{All } 3\, 2, \text{All } 2\, 1\} \models \varphi$.

What are the possible choices for $\varphi$? Answer, making sure to thoroughly justify why the sentences you gave are the only ones which work.

### Answer
Suppose $φ$ is All x y for $x, y \in \{1, 2, 3\}$. If $x < y$ then $\{\text{All 3 2, All 2 1}\}̸ |= φ$. If $x > y$ then

   $\{\text{All 1 2, All 2 3}\}̸ |= φ$. Hence $x = y$, in which case both bullet points are clearly satisfied. So we only

   have the 3 sentences of the form All x x for $x \in \{1, 2, 3\}$.

## Open Question 5
> Work in the logic $\mathcal{A}$, over any signature. We say a model $\mathcal{M}$ in the signature $P$ is *fluffy* in case $[\![p]\!]$ is nonempty for every noun $p \in P$. Define $\Gamma \models^f \varphi$ in case every fluffy model satisfying $\Gamma$ must also satisfy $\varphi$.
>
> Now it's certainly true that if $\Gamma \models \varphi$ then $\Gamma \models^f \varphi$ as well. (This has nothing to do with fluffiness, it's just a general fact that entailment over a restricted class of models is a more permissive relation.) But for this question you must prove the converse, that is, assuming that $\Gamma \models^f \varphi$, prove that $\Gamma \models \varphi$.

(Note that unlike examples we saw in class and the practice problems where the converse relation was *false*, here the converse relation is *true*. Your hint is, given an arbitrary model, can you make it fluffy without changing the sentences it satisfies or falsifies?)

### Answer
Assume that $Γ |= f φ$. Our goal is to show $Γ |= φ$. So suppose $M$ is an arbitrary model that

   satisfies $Γ$. Our goal is now to show that $M$ satisfies $φ$.

   $M$ may not be fluffy. But we can easily define a fluffy “one-point extension” of $M$ as follows. In

   English: we add a single point to the domain of $M$, and we add this new point to the denotations of each of the nouns as well. In mathematics: our new domain is $M ∪ \{⋆\}$, where $⋆$ is a new point. The denotation of $p$ in the new model is the denotation of $p$ in the old model plus the point $⋆$, for each noun p. Call this model $M⋆$. It is fluffy because the denotation of every sentence letter contains at least this new point $\{⋆\}$.

   Notice that $M$ and $M⋆$ satisfy exactly the same sentences! This is because for any two sets $X$ and $Y$ and

   any point $⋆$ not in either of them, $X ⊆ Y ⇐⇒ X ∪ \{⋆\} ⊆ Y ∪ \{⋆\}$. Hence $M⋆ |= Γ$. But since $Γ |= f φ$,

   $M⋆ |= φ$. But then $M |= φ$, which is what we wanted to show in the first place.

## Open Question 6
> Work in a general logical system with a notion of model and proof that has both soundness and completeness. Let $\mathcal{M}_\Gamma$ be the canonical model of a set of sentences $\Gamma$. This means that $\Gamma \vdash \varphi \iff \mathcal{M}_\Gamma \models \varphi$. Is it also true that $\Gamma \models \varphi \iff \mathcal{M}_\Gamma \models \varphi$? There are two parts to this question:

(a) If $\Gamma \models \varphi$, must $\mathcal{M}_\Gamma \models \varphi$? Either prove or give a counterexample.

(b) If $\mathcal{M}_\Gamma \models \varphi$, must $\Gamma \models \varphi$? Either prove or give a counterexample.

Again, we can work entirely on the level of a general logic as outlined in the first page of this exam. Remember you can assume this logic has soundness and completeness.

### Answer
To part (a)—yes. This has nothing to do with canonicity specifically, and applies to all models

   of $Γ$. Since $MΓ |= Γ$ and every model of $Γ$ also models $φ$, $MΓ |= φ$.

   To part (b)—yes as well, but now we use canonicity. Suppose $MΓ |= φ$. Then, by property of being a

   canonical model, $Γ ⊢ φ$, and by soundness, $Γ |= φ$

## Open Question 8
Express the relation $\min(a, b) < \max(c, d)$ by a first-order formula in the variables $a, b, c, d$ that only uses the relation $<$. Explain carefully why your answer is correct.

(*Hint.* For example, $x < \min(y, z) \iff (x < y) \land (x < z)$. In other words, a number is less than the minimum of two numbers iff it's less than *both* of them.)

### Answer
$\min(a, b) < \max(c, d)$ is satisfied exactly when one of $\{a, b\}$ is less than one of $\{c, d\}$. In other

   words,

   $a < c \lor a < d \lor b < c \lor b < d$.
